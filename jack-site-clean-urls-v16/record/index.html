Abstract: 1 minFull read: 3 min

Levelling Up investment for Arbroath was at risk of being redirected. Jack engaged at national level to protect the allocation and now sits on the Arbroath Town Board with direct oversight of how every pound is spent and what it produces.

What happened

The £20 million Levelling Up allocation for Arbroath represented the most significant single investment in the town in a generation. When it became clear that the allocation was not secure and that without active advocacy it could be redirected elsewhere, Jack moved quickly — engaging directly with both the council administration and with national Conservative contacts to make the case for Arbroath and ensure the money stayed where it had been promised.

The investment was protected. Jack was subsequently appointed to the Arbroath Town Board, which has direct oversight of how the funds are committed and spent. That appointment is not ceremonial. It gives Jack a formal, active role in scrutinising whether the money is producing what was promised — not just whether projects are being delivered on schedule, but whether they are genuinely changing conditions in the town.

The practical standard Jack applies on the Town Board is simple: is the money producing visible, lasting improvements to Arbroath's town centre, infrastructure and community facilities that residents can see and use? Where the answer is uncertain or the evidence unclear, he pushes for more specific reporting and clearer milestones. Investment that produces process rather than outcome is not success. In a town that has waited a long time for this level of funding, every pound has to count.

The broader lesson from securing this investment is one Jack applies across his work: if you do not make the active case for what your community needs, someone else will make the case for what they need instead. Arbroath's allocation did not survive because it was the obvious choice — it survived because someone was willing to fight for it. That is what representation means in practice.

Abstract: 1 minFull read: 3 min

Drove the development of a road reporting and accountability system now recognised as among the most advanced of its kind in the UK. Faster responses, proper accountability, and measurable outcomes — not just for Arbroath West, but across the whole of Angus.

What happened

Road surface conditions in Arbroath West were a persistent source of resident frustration and a legitimate daily test of whether the council was performing its most basic maintenance function. The existing process for reporting and tracking road defects was structurally inadequate — issues were reported, logged and too often allowed to sit unresolved without meaningful follow-up or accountability to the person who reported them.

Jack drove the development and implementation of a system integrating public reporting with internal performance monitoring in a way that makes it materially harder for issues to be reported and then ignored. Reports are tracked through to resolution. Response times are measured. The data is available for scrutiny. When repairs fall outside expected timescales, there is a visible record of that — one which Jack uses in committee to ask specific questions about why specific failures occurred and what is being done to prevent their recurrence.

The resulting system is among the most advanced road accountability frameworks operated by a Scottish local authority. Its practical effect has been faster repairs, fewer repeat reports of the same issues, and a measurable improvement in the standard of road maintenance across the ward. Officers who know their response times are being tracked perform differently to officers who do not. That is not a cynical observation — it is how accountability produces improvement in practice.

Jack's position on infrastructure is that it is the most visible test of whether a council is doing its job. Residents do not need to read committee reports to know whether their roads are in good condition. They experience that question every day. When the answer is consistently no, the council's credibility on everything else is undermined. That is why this work matters, and why maintaining pressure on delivery standards is a standing part of how Jack operates in committee.

Abstract: 1 minFull read: 3 min

As Vice-Convenor of the Communities Committee, Jack has driven a shift in how the committee operates — from one that received reports and noted them, to one that challenges underperformance, demands specific data and holds officers accountable for measurable outcomes.

What happened

The Communities Committee has oversight of a broad range of local services — from housing and community facilities to environmental services and local regeneration. That oversight function only has value if exercised with genuine rigour. When a committee receives a report, notes it and moves on without probing the data or following up on commitments made at previous sessions, it is not performing scrutiny. It is performing the appearance of scrutiny. Jack's approach since taking on the Vice-Convenor role has been to insist on the real thing.

In practice, that has meant examining the underlying data rather than accepting summary statements at face value. When performance figures are presented in aggregate, Jack requests disaggregated breakdowns that might reveal variation between areas or service types that the headline numbers obscure. When officers present point-in-time snapshots, he requests trend data so the committee can see whether performance is improving, static or declining over time. When commitments made at previous committee sessions have not been honoured, he raises them explicitly rather than allowing them to quietly disappear from the agenda.

The cumulative effect of that sustained pressure is a committee that officers know they must come prepared for. The quality of performance reporting presented to the Communities Committee has improved during this period — not because the council suddenly became more transparent by choice, but because the committee created conditions in which incomplete or unclear reporting produced specific, uncomfortable questions. That is what effective scrutiny looks like, and it is the standard Jack intends to maintain for as long as he holds this role.

The broader principle is one Jack applies across all his committee work: accountability is not an event, it is a condition. It has to be maintained consistently, across multiple sessions, to change institutional behaviour. A single hard question does not change how an organisation reports. A sustained pattern of hard questions, session after session, does. That requires patience and attention to detail that Jack considers a core part of the job.

Abstract: 1 minFull read: 3 min

Pushed for active council intervention on town centre vacancy — challenging the passive approach and arguing for direct engagement with landlords, creative use of planning powers, and investment that produces lasting commercial activity, not just improved streetscapes.

What happened

Empty units accelerate decline. When significant proportions of retail frontage are vacant, the remaining businesses suffer reduced footfall, the overall impression of the town centre deteriorates for visitors and potential investors alike, and the cycle of decline becomes self-reinforcing. Jack's position is that the council has tools available to address this and has not been using them with sufficient energy or ambition.

The approach Jack has pushed for in committee goes beyond waiting for market conditions to change. It includes direct engagement with landlords who hold vacant units — understanding whether the barrier is commercial, legal or structural, and working with the council's planning and economic development functions to identify whether public intervention can unlock occupation. In some cases that means facilitating change of use. In others, it means using the council's own estate and investment powers to seed activity that attracts further commercial interest.

The Levelling Up investment Jack helped secure for Arbroath creates the most significant single opportunity to change the trajectory of the town centre in a generation. His position on the Arbroath Town Board means he has direct influence over how that money is targeted. His expectation is that it produces lasting changes in footfall, commercial activity and the physical condition of the town centre — not ribbon-cutting moments that look good in a press release but produce no lasting economic activity once the initial investment has been absorbed.

The measure Jack applies to town centre investment is straightforward: in five years, is Arbroath's high street materially better? Are there fewer vacant units? Is there more commercial activity? Are more people choosing to spend time and money there? If the answer to those questions is not convincingly yes, the investment has not been directed well enough. That standard — judging investment by its actual effect on conditions rather than its delivery — is the one Jack intends to apply throughout his time on the Town Board.

Abstract: 1 minFull read: 3 min

As a Director of Carnoustie Golf Links, Jack has pushed to ensure that one of Scotland's most globally recognised tourism assets generates tangible economic benefit for Arbroath — a question of connectivity, marketing and the quality of what visitors find when they arrive.

What happened

Carnoustie Golf Links is a venue of global standing. The Open Championship, when held at Carnoustie, brings the area a level of international attention and visitor traffic that most Scottish towns never experience. The question Jack has raised consistently since joining the board is whether Arbroath captures a meaningful share of the economic activity that attention generates. At present, it does not — not to anything like the degree it could. Visitors to Carnoustie do not routinely extend their stay to include Arbroath, and the connectivity between the two — in transport, in joint marketing, in accommodation and in the quality of what a visitor would find — is not currently good enough to make that extension feel natural or worthwhile.

Addressing this is partly a marketing problem, partly an infrastructure problem and partly a problem of the quality of the visitor offer in Arbroath itself. Jack's position is that all three require active attention rather than the assumption that Arbroath will benefit from proximity to Carnoustie by default. The board has a role in how Carnoustie positions itself nationally and internationally, and Jack has used his position on it to push for a more deliberate strategy of extending the economic footprint of the Links into the wider area.

Beyond the Carnoustie connection, Arbroath has cultural and historical assets that are underexploited as tourism draws in their own right. The Arbroath Declaration of 1320 is a document of genuine national significance. The abbey is an atmospheric and historically important site. The coastline is among the most dramatic in eastern Scotland. A coherent tourism strategy that connected these assets with the golf offer at Carnoustie, and invested in the quality of what visitors encounter when they arrive, would produce significantly better economic outcomes than the current fragmented approach.

The Tourism priority in Jack's political platform is not separate from his work on the Golf Links board — it is the same argument applied at different levels of intervention. At the board, he pushes for strategic decisions that extend the economic reach of Carnoustie. In the council chamber, he pushes for investment decisions that raise the quality of what Arbroath offers as a destination. The goal in both cases is the same: more visitors staying longer and spending more, with the economic benefit landing in Arbroath rather than passing through it.

Abstract: 1 minFull read: 3 min

Pushed consistently across every committee for clearer performance reporting and greater visibility of outcomes — making accountability a standing expectation rather than something that happens when scrutiny is applied externally.

What happened

Transparency is not a soft governance value. It is the mechanism through which accountability becomes real. When residents can see clearly how their money is being spent and whether commitments are being honoured, the incentive structure within the institution changes. Officers who know their work will be measured and reported publicly are subject to a different kind of accountability than those who are not. Jack has treated the raising of this standard as one of the most important things he can do in his committee roles, precisely because it creates the conditions for every other kind of improvement.

In practice, that has meant pushing for performance data to be presented in specific, disaggregated, time-series form rather than as point-in-time headline numbers that can be selected to present the most favourable picture. It has meant requesting that commitments made in committee be formally minuted and tracked, so that the record is clear when they have and have not been honoured. And it has meant resisting the tendency — common in committee settings — to move on from an issue once an officer has provided a plausible explanation, rather than following up to see whether the explanation proved accurate.

The cumulative effect of this approach across multiple committees and multiple years has been a council that reports with more specificity than it did before Jack held these roles. That is a genuine institutional improvement, even if it is invisible to most residents. The mechanism by which it produces better outcomes is straightforward: specific, public performance data creates a feedback loop that general narrative reporting does not. When a council publishes that response times for road repairs averaged 47 days last quarter, that number is subject to comparison, questioning and political pressure. When it reports that the Roads team is working hard to address maintenance challenges, it is not.

Jack intends to continue applying this standard for as long as he holds committee positions, and to use it to push for specific improvements rather than accepting general assurances of progress. The principle is simple: what gets measured gets managed, and what gets reported publicly gets taken seriously. That principle applies to roads, to schools, to social care and to every other service the council provides.
